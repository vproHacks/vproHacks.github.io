THE ART OF
THE RETRO
REBRAND

DATE MAY 14, 2024
READ TIME 8 MINUTES
AUTHOR SIGNAL STUDIO
WORDS ~1,400

There's something happening in design right now that feels both nostalgic and genuinely new. Across social media, a generation of creators is taking the world's most recognizable brands — the Googles, the Netflixes, the Instagrams — and reimagining them through the lens of 1980s CRT television. The results are remarkable.

The key figure in this movement is @KXDGraphics, whose retro-futuristic rebrand work has accumulated millions of views. But what makes these designs resonate so deeply, and what can the rest of us learn from them?

[ ARTICLE IMAGE — 1600 × 900px ]

WHY CRT WORKS

The CRT monitor wasn't just a display technology — it was a complete visual culture. The phosphor glow, the scanline texture, the gentle rounding of corners, the way colors bled into each other at the edges. These weren't bugs. They were, in retrospect, a deeply coherent aesthetic.

Today's screens are perfect. Every pixel lands exactly where it should. The result is a visual flatness that, paradoxically, feels less real than the imperfect analog image it replaced. We've traded warmth for precision.

"THE SOFT CRT BLUR PULLS EVERYTHING TOGETHER, GIVING EACH LOGO THAT SLIGHTLY IMPERFECT, FLICKERING FEEL."
— Bored Panda, April 2026

What KXD understood before most is that the imperfections are the point. The scanlines aren't a flaw to be corrected — they're a texture that gives the image physical presence. The chromatic aberration isn't a defect — it's depth.

IMPLEMENTING THE STYLE

The CRT aesthetic breaks down into reproducible components: scanlines, phosphor glow, chromatic aberration and the characteristic palette of early television — deep blacks, vibrant primaries and that distinctive warm white that only CRT phosphors produce.

In CSS, a convincing scanline layer needs only a repeating gradient. The trick is keeping the opacity barely perceptible — enough to feel like a texture, not a filter:

/* Scanlines — keep opacity below 0.08 */
body::after {
  content: '';
  position: fixed;
  inset: 0;
  background: repeating-linear-gradient(
    0deg,
    transparent 2px,
    rgba(0,0,0,0.055) 3px
  );
  pointer-events: none;
  z-index: 9999;
}

THE TYPOGRAPHY TREATMENT

One of the most distinctive elements of the KXD style is the typographic hierarchy: massive, heavy display type for the primary word, paired with widely-spaced small caps for the subtitle below. The scale contrast is enormous — the headline might be 8–10× the size of the label beneath it.

This mirrors how television graphics were composed in the 80s: bold and readable at distance, designed for a screen where fine detail was lost to scanline blur. Bebas Neue, Barlow Condensed Black and similar compressed display faces work particularly well. For the widest letter-spacing on subtitles, target 0.35–0.5em.

COLOR & THE RAINBOW BAR

The rainbow horizontal stripe is the single most recognizable element in KXD's CRT icon pack. In the context of 1980s television, these rainbow color bars were the broadcast standard test pattern — used to calibrate screens before content played. Repurposed as a design element, they immediately signal the aesthetic.

In web design, a 3–5px animated rainbow bar used as a section separator or page accent is immediately recognizable. Keep the saturation high and the opacity full — these bars work because the colors feel genuinely on, like a lit display, not a printed ink.

MAKING IT YOUR OWN

The danger with any trend is imitation without understanding. The best retro-influenced work isn't costume design — it's a genuine reckoning with what made those aesthetics effective, translated into something new. Study the originals. Understand the constraints that created them. Then break the rules deliberately.

The phosphor glow, the scanlines, the rainbow stripes — none of these are mandatory. Use what serves the concept and discard the rest. The goal is to capture a feeling, not replicate a technology. The best CRT-inspired designs feel like they could only exist now — aware of history, but not trapped in it.
